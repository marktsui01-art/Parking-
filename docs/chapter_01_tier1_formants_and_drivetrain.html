1.1 DSP From Zero: Waves, Harmonics, and Why Your Current Synth Buzzes

Figure 1.1: Three-Stage Procedural Engine Audio Architecture

What Sound Actually Is in Code

Sound in air is tiny, rapid fluctuations in atmospheric pressure pushing on your eardrum. Your browser's Web Audio API represents this pressure wave digitally as a stream of floating-point numbers x[n] ∈ [-1.0, +1.0], typically sampled f_s = 48,000 times per second (48 kHz).

Every periodic sound can be viewed in two complementary ways:

  • Time Domain (x[n] vs. time): The shape of the pressure wiggle from millisecond to millisecond (what an oscilloscope draws).
  • Frequency Domain (Amplitude vs. Hz): The recipe of pure sine waves that add together to make that shape (what the FFT bar graph in src/audio_tuner.html draws).

Before touching filters, keep two perceptual rules of human hearing in mind—both are logarithmic, not linear:

  1. Pitch is perceived in Octaves (2× frequency): Going from 50 Hz → 100 Hz sounds like the exact same musical interval (one octave) as going from 1,000 Hz → 2,000 Hz.

  2. Loudness is measured in Decibels (dB):

    dB = 20 log_10((A_2 / A_1))

    • +6 dB ≈ 2× the wave amplitude.
    • -6 dB ≈ 0.5× the wave amplitude.
    • -20 dB = 0.1× the wave amplitude (10% left).
    • -40 dB = 0.01× the wave amplitude (1% left—nearly inaudible in a mix).

Fourier's Theorem & The Harmonic Series

In 1822, Joseph Fourier proved a remarkable fact: any repeating waveform with fundamental frequency f_0 (say, 50 Hz) is mathematically identical to a sum of pure sine waves at integer multiples of f_0:

1f_0, 2f_0, 3f_0, 4f_0, 5f_0 …

These multiples are called harmonics (or overtones). What distinguishes a flute from a brass horn or a buzzing chainsaw playing the exact same 50 Hz pitch is solely the relative loudness of those harmonics:

Web Audio Waveform Which Harmonics Exist? Natural High-End Roll-Off What It Sounds Like Raw
sine Only 1f_0 (no overtones) Infinite (nothing above f_0) Pure tuning fork, hum, sub-bass
triangle Odd only (1f_0, 3f_0, 5f_0 …) Fast: amplitude ∝ (1 / n^2) (-12 dB/oct) Mellow, hollow, muted flute
square Odd only (1f_0, 3f_0, 5f_0 …) Slow: amplitude ∝ (1 / n) (-6 dB/oct) Clarinet, 8-bit Nintendo
sawtooth All (1f_0, 2f_0, 3f_0, 4f_0 …) Slow: amplitude ∝ (1 / n) (-6 dB/oct) Bright, raspy, buzzy synthesizer

Engine Orders vs. Arbitrary baseFreq

In automotive acoustics, engineers don't guess a magic baseFreq: 17 or baseFreq: 22—every frequency comes directly from crankshaft geometry:

  • Crankshaft Rotational Frequency (1st Engine Order, or 1E):

    f_rot = (RPM / 60) Hz

  • Single-Cylinder Firing Frequency (0.5E, the "Half-Order"): In a 4-stroke engine (Intake → Compression → Power → Exhaust), each cylinder fires once every two crank revolutions:

    f_cyl = 0.5 · f_rot = (RPM / 120) Hz

  • Total Engine Firing Frequency ((N_cyl / 2)E): Across N_cyl cylinders firing evenly over two revolutions:

    f_fire = (N_cyl / 2) · f_rot = (RPM · N_cyl / 120) Hz

Stratified Waveforms, Order Hierarchy & Crankshaft Phase-Locking

Knowing which engine orders exist (1.0E, 1.5E, 3.0E …) is only half the source problem. When you actually rev an engine across a 5×–8× RPM span, three physical rules govern whether those oscillators sound like a real cast-metal powertrain or a buzzy, warbling synthesizer:

1. Mid-RPM "Spectral Evacuation" vs. High-RPM Sub-Order Dominance

When an engine sweeps from 900 RPM (f_rot = 15 Hz) up to 4,500 RPM (f_rot = 75 Hz), every harmonic oscillator shifts upward by 5× (more than two octaves).

  • If you omit 1.0x and 1.5x: On a Flat-6, 3.0x climbs to 225 Hz at 4,500 RPM, leaving the entire 50–150 Hz chest register completely empty ("spectral evacuation"). Psychoacoustically, the moment low-mid chest energy disappears while 1–3 kHz overtones remain, your brain stops hearing a heavy aluminum engine block and hears a thin synth lead.
  • If 1.0x and 1.5x stay too loud at redline: Conversely, on an inherently balanced, even-firing engine (such as a horizontally opposed Flat-6 or a 72° Formula 1 V10), primary reciprocating forces cancel. At 7,400 RPM, the merged primary firing order (3.0x = 370 Hz on a Flat-6; 5.0x = 1,583 Hz on a 19,000 RPM V10) must dominate the spectrum by +5 to +15 dB over 1.0x! If 1.0x and 1.5x grow linearly to high gain at redline, they boom unnaturally and consume the headroom of your downstream nonlinear waveshaper—ducking and compressing the high-frequency "zing" every time the low bass wave peaks!
  • Rule of thumb: Anchor the chest floor with moderate 1.0x and single-bank (1.5x on 6-cyl, 2.5x on V10) orders at low/mid RPM, then give 1.0x (and 1.5x on even-fire engines) a flat or slightly negative RPM gain slope (rpmModGain <= 0) so they step gracefully out of the way as the primary firing order (3.0x / 5.0x) and upper overtones (6.0x, 9.0x) take command! (Exception: odd-firing engines with divorced bank pipes—such as an 8.4L 90° V10 with 54° / 90° split crankpins and side-exit exhausts—physically retain massive 1.0x, 1.5x, and 2.5x 5-cylinder bank energy all the way to redline.)

2. Waveform-by-Order Stratification (1/n^2 vs. 1/n)

Never assign sawtooth blindly to every engine order! Stratify waveforms by their physical acoustic job:

Order Tier Example Orders Best Waveform Physical & Spectral Reason
Sub & Bank Undertones 1.0x (crank/block), 1.5x (3-cyl bank), 2.5x (5-cyl bank) triangle (-12 dB/oct) Odd harmonics decay fast as 1/n^2, injecting pure 50–250 Hz chest weight without polluting 2–6 kHz with extra sawtooth buzz.
Primary Firing Core 2.0x (I4), 3.0x (I6/Flat-6), 4.0x (V8), 5.0x (V10) sawtooth (-6 dB/oct) Contains all integer harmonics (1/n) to excite mid and upper exhaust formants as RPM climbs.

3. Crankshaft Phase-Locking (0 cents Detune) vs. The Chorus Warble Trap

In music synthesizers, sound designers often detune oscillators by ± 4 to 10 cents ("supersaw") to make pads sound wide. Never detune harmonic orders of a single engine!

Physically, every piston and exhaust valve in an engine is geared to one rigid steel crankshaft—the left cylinder bank cannot spin at 6,500 RPM while the right bank spins at 6,515 RPM! If you detune 1.5x by -4 cents and 4.5x by +5 cents before a nonlinear waveshaper, their harmonics drift in and out of phase 2–4 times per second, producing an 18% slow amplitude "wah-wah" warble (like a guitar chorus pedal or an uneven twin-engine prop plane). Keep all crankshaft orders 100% phase-locked (detune = 0); real cycle-to-cycle mechanical life comes from stochastic gas turbulence inside the waveshaper (Section 1.4), not detuned pitch beating!

1.2 Digital Filters & The Suspension Analogy (f_c, Slope, and Q)

Subtractive synthesis works just like sculpting marble: you start with a harmonically rich raw wave (lots of overtones) and carve away the frequencies a physical muffler, intake box, and engine block wouldn't let out.

In Web Audio, ctx.createBiquadFilter() gives you a 2nd-order digital filter ("biquad" because its transfer function is a ratio of two quadratic polynomials—under the hood, it computes each output sample using just 5 multiplications on the current/last 2 input samples and last 2 output samples).

The Suspension Analogy: What f_c and Q Actually Mean

Figure 1.2: 2nd-Order vs. Cascaded 4th-Order (-24 dB/oct) Low-Pass Filter Response & Web Audio Q Conventions

If you understand how coilover springs and dampers behave on a car, you already understand a 2nd-order lowpass filter—they obey the exact same 2nd-order differential equation!

Imagine driving over bumps of frequency f, where road height is the input wave and car body motion is the output wave:

  1. Cutoff Frequency (f_c, filter.frequency.value in Hz): This is the natural frequency of the spring-mass system (ω_n = √k/m).

    • Slow highway undulations (f ≪ f_c): The chassis follows the road 1:1 (Passband, 0 dB).
    • Fast cobblestone chatter (f ≫ f_c): The tire vibrates, but the heavy chassis doesn't have time to move. Above f_c, a single 2nd-order lowpass filter attenuates energy at -12 dB per octave (every time frequency doubles, amplitude drops by 4×!).

  2. Quality Factor (Q, filter.Q.value) & Web Audio's Crucial Unit Law: In textbook physics (Robert Bristow-Johnson's Audio EQ Cookbook), dimensionless quality factor Q = (1 / 2ζ) governs resonance: Q = (1 / √2) ≈ 0.707 is Butterworth critical damping (maximally flat passband with zero overshoot). However, Web Audio's BiquadFilterNode uses two different units for Q depending on filter.type:

    • For peaking, bandpass, and notch, filter.Q.value is dimensionless Q directly (0.707 = wide, 4.0 = narrow).
    • For lowpass and highpass, Web Audio defines filter.Q.value in decibels! To convert dimensionless textbook Q into Web Audio's lowpass/highpass parameter, always apply:
      Q_WebAudio (dB) = 20 log_10(Q_dimensionless)
      Thus, true maximally flat Butterworth damping (Q = 0.707) requires setting filter.Q.value = -3.01 dB (Q = 1.0 maps to 0.0 dB, and Q = 2.0 maps to +6.02 dB of resonant peaking)!

Why Cascading Two Butterworth Low-Pass Filters (-24 dB/oct) Rounds the Waveform

Why does a single 2nd-order lowpass filter still sound slightly "synthy" in the midrange (3,000–5,000 RPM) once f_c opens up to 700–1,200 Hz?

By Fourier's theorem, "buzziness" in the frequency domain is mathematically identical to sharp vertical step discontinuities (high sample-to-sample slew |dx/dt|) in the time domain:

  • A raw sawtooth decays slowly at -6 dB/oct. Passing it through one 2nd-order low-pass (-12 dB/oct) yields -18 dB/oct—gentle enough that 2.5–6 kHz sawtooth teeth remain audible whenever f_c opens under throttle.
  • Wiring two critically damped Butterworth low-pass filters in series (lp1 -> lp2, Q ≈ 0.707–0.75) doubles the slope to -24 dB/octave (-30 dB/oct total roll-off on a sawtooth!) with zero resonant bump at f_c. In the time domain, this 4th-order cascade acts as a physical spline smoother that rounds every sharp sawtooth corner into a continuous, organic exhaust pressure pulse!

1.3 What Is a Formant Filter? (The Source–Filter Model)

Figure 1.3: Source-Filter Model (Stratified Harmonic Orders × Wide-Q Body Dome & Zing Formant)

This brings us to your core question: What actually is a formant filter, and why is it the secret to realistic acoustic synthesis?

The Human Voice Experiment

Sing the vowel "Ahhh" at a low note, and then slide your pitch smoothly up an octave while still holding "Ahhh". Now repeat the exact same low-to-high pitch slide while singing "Eeee".

Notice two things happened simultaneously:

  1. The Source (Your Vocal Cords) slid up in frequency (f_0, 2f_0, 3f_0 … all doubled).
  2. The Filter (Your Throat & Mouth Cavity) did NOT shrink to half its physical size when you sang higher! Because the physical volume and length of your vocal tract stayed constant, its acoustic resonant peaks—called Formants (F_1 ≈ 730 Hz, F_2 ≈ 1,090 Hz for "Ahhh"; F_1 ≈ 270 Hz, F_2 ≈ 2,290 Hz for "Eeee")—stayed fixed in Hz!

Definition: A Formant is a stationary (or geometry-dependent, not pitch-dependent) resonant peak of a physical acoustic cavity.

Why Moving Filters Sound Like Cartoon Synths

Look at line 374 of your current AudioController.js:

intakeFreq: p.intake.filterBase + (absSpeed * p.intake.filterRange)

Right now, as absSpeed goes from 0 to 1, your intake bandpass filter slides from 400 Hz up to 2900 Hz in lockstep with RPM. Physically, that simulates an intake airbox that magically shrinks from the size of a shoebox at idle down to the size of a thimble at redline! When both harmonics and filter peaks move together, your ear hears a synthetic "wah-wah" pitch-shift rather than a real metal machine.

In a real car, the engine block, intake plenum, intake runners, header collectors, and muffler chambers have fixed physical dimensions:

  1. Helmholtz Resonances (100–350 Hz): Just like blowing across the neck of an empty glass bottle, air mass in an intake snorkel bouncing against the springiness of air inside the airbox (or a muffler expansion chamber) rings at a fixed low-mid frequency:

    f_H = (c / 2π)√(A_neck / V_cavity · L_neck)

  2. Pipe Standing-Wave Modes (400–2,500 Hz): Intake runners (30–50 cm) and exhaust mid-pipes (1.5–3 m) reinforce fixed wavelengths (f = (c / 4L) or (c / 2L)).

  3. What creates the "Spine-Tingling Sweep": As RPM climbs, the engine's harmonics (1×, 2×, 3×, 4× f_fire) march upward through those fixed formant peaks one after another. Each time a strong engine harmonic crosses a stationary cavity formant, that harmonic suddenly swells and sings before passing through and handing off to the next harmonic below it!

(Note: The one mechanical exception where an intake formant jumps between two fixed frequencies is a physical variable intake resonance flap—like Porsche's dual-stage resonance intake manifold on the 987.2 S or BMW's DISA valve—which snaps open a connecting plenum valve around 4,200–5,500 RPM, shifting the Helmholtz peak between two discrete physical states!)

Exhaust Thermodynamics: Why Exhaust Formants Drift Gently (20–45%) With Load & RPM

Look closely at the Helmholtz and standing-wave equations above (f_H ∝ c and f_pipe = (c / 4L)): both depend linearly on the local speed of sound c inside the gas! In an ideal gas, the speed of sound is governed by absolute temperature T (in Kelvin):

c = √γ R_specific T
  • Intake Tract (Cold Ambient Air, Stationary Formants): Fresh air entering the airbox and intake runners stays near ambient temperature (T ≈ 295–315 K, so c ≈ 345–355 m/s regardless of RPM). Intake cavity formants are therefore genuinely stationary in Hz.
  • Exhaust System (Hot Combustion Gas, Controlled Thermal Drift): Exhaust gas leaves the ports at ~ 570 K (300°C) at idle/light cruise (c ≈ 480 m/s), but climbs to ~ 1,100–1,200 K (830–930°C) under sustained high-RPM wide-open throttle (c ≈ 670–700 m/s)! Because √1150 / 570 ≈ 1.42, physical exhaust formants naturally drift upward in frequency by 20–45% with RPM and throttle load (for instance, a 275 Hz warm chest resonance rising gently toward ~ 365 Hz at WOT redline)—far smaller than a 500% cartoon synth sweep, yet crucial for conveying rising exhaust heat and gas velocity!

Wide-Q "Body Domes" vs. Narrow Synth Spikes & The Mid-Scoop Trap

For a 2nd-order peaking filter, its -3 dB frequency bandwidth Δ f and its time-domain impulse ringing decay constant τ are coupled directly through Q:

Δ f = (f_c / Q), τ ≈ (Q / π f_c)

Two classic EQ mistakes immediately make a procedural engine sound thin or synthetic between 3,000 and 6,000 RPM:

  1. Narrow High-Q Peaks (Q > 4.0): A narrow peak has a long ringing tail τ. Whenever an engine harmonic sweeps across a +10 dB, Q = 5 spike in the sensitive 1–2 kHz region, it whistles like a resonant flute or acid-house synth filter.
  2. Over-Scooping the Lower Midrange (250–400 Hz): Studio engineers sometimes cut 300–350 Hz on guitar tracks to reduce "boxiness." On a 6-cylinder engine, however, 3.0x (f_fire) spends the entire 3,000–6,000 RPM midrange walking straight through 150–300 Hz while 4.5x traverses 225–450 Hz! Placing a -4.5 dB notch at 330 Hz hollows out the engine's primary firing note right where it needs mechanical chest weight.

The Wide-Q Body Dome Solution: Place two broad, low-Q (Q = 1.2–1.6) positive peaking filters at adjacent low-mid frequencies—e.g., F_1 ≈ 140–150 Hz (+6 dB, Q = 1.3) and F_2 ≈ 265–285 Hz (+5 dB, Q = 1.4). Because their wide bell curves (Δ f ≈ 110–200 Hz) overlap smoothly with almost zero time-domain ringing, they fuse into a continuous +5 to +6.5 dB acoustic body plateau spanning 80–450 Hz—giving cast-iron V8s, side-pipe V10s, and aluminum Flat-6 blocks unmistakable physical thickness across the entire rev range.

Nonlinear Cam-Crossover Curves (u_RPM^γ) & Top-End Metallic "Zing"

Figure 1.4: Convex Cam-Crossover Curve (u_RPM^2.8) & Stratified Engine-Order Gain Envelopes Across RPM

Suppose you modulate your dual -24 dB/oct exhaust low-pass cutoff linearly with normalized RPM u_RPM ∈ [0, 1]:

u_RPM = ((RPM - RPM_idle) / (RPM_redline - RPM_idle))

You immediately hit a frustrating wall:

  • If you set the redline cutoff high enough (3,500–4,300 Hz) to let out the exhilarating 2.0–4.5 kHz metallic "zing" and raspy scream at 7,400 RPM, linear scaling forces the cutoff wide open to ~ 1,750 Hz already at 3,500 RPM—making cruising midrange buzzy and thin!
  • Conversely, if you clamp redline cutoff down to 1,800 Hz to protect midrange warmth, the top end (6,000–7,400 RPM) sounds dark and suffocated!

How does a real naturally aspirated sports engine (Porsche VarioCam Plus, Honda VTEC, BMW Double-VANOS) stay warm and rounded at 3,500 RPM yet erupt into a metallic scream above 5,500 RPM? Nonlinear valvetrain breathing! Below 4,500 RPM, the engine breathes through mild, short-duration cam lobes; in the upper third of the tach, high-lift cam profiles and resonance flaps open, dramatically accelerating exhaust blowdown slew rate (dp/dt).

In DSP, we model this cleanly by raising u_RPM to a convex power-law exponent γ > 1 (γ ≈ 2.2–2.8):

p(u_RPM, u_load) = p_base + Δ p_RPM · u_RPM^γ + Δ p_load · u_load

Check the numbers at 3,500 RPM (u_RPM = 0.40 on a 900–7,400 RPM Flat-6) with γ = 2.8:

  • u_RPM^2.8 = 0.40^2.8 = 0.077 (less than 8% of the RPM modulation has engaged!). Even with a huge +2,650 Hz high-RPM cutoff span, f_LP stays tucked around ~ 700 Hz at 3,500 RPM—100% warm, rounded, and buzz-free.
  • Above 5,800 RPM (u_RPM = 0.75 → 1.00), u_RPM^2.8 accelerates steeply from 0.45 → 1.00, sweeping the dual low-pass out to 3,300–4,350 Hz right as upper sawtooth zing harmonics (6.0x with γ = 2.2, 9.0x with γ = 2.6) and a dedicated High Metallic Zing Formant (1,650 → 2,650 Hz, +6.5 dB, Q = 2.2) blossom into a spine-tingling mechanical scream!

  1. Pre-Waveshaper Stochastic Intermodulation (sech^2 Turbulence Coupling): Pure oscillators are 100% periodic (s(t+T) = s(t)), whereas real cylinders exhibit cycle-to-cycle combustion variations and high-velocity port shear. If you layer noise after your filters, your ear hears static radio hiss sitting on top of a clean synth. A first-order Taylor expansion reveals the magic:
    y(t) = tanh(k · (s(t) + n(t))) ≈ tanh(k · s(t)) [Saturated Engine Wave] + k · sech^2(k · s(t)) · n(t) [Pitch-Synchronized Turbulent Bursts]

Because sech^2(x) = 1 - tanh^2(x) peaks at every waveform zero-crossing and compresses near wave crests, the nonlinearity automatically amplitude-modulates the gas noise in lockstep with each cylinder firing pulse! (Headroom corollary: this same sech^2 term also explains why overly loud 1.0x bass waves compress and duck 2–4.5 kHz zing harmonics inside the waveshaper—keep 1.0x balanced at high RPM!)

  2. Series peaking Formant Bank (4 Zones) + Dual -24 dB/oct Butterworth Low-Pass: Four series peaking biquads take < 0.1% CPU and map 1-to-1 onto physical acoustic regions:

    • F_1 (100–160 Hz, Q ≈ 1.2–1.5): Lower Body / Block Thump (displacement chest weight).

Suppose on your Porsche 987.2 S (flat6), you place a sharp resonant formant peak at F_3 = 1,050 Hz (Q = 4.5). Remember from Pause & Think 1.1 that the Flat-6's fundamental 6-cylinder firing frequency is f_fire = 3 f_rot = (RPM / 20) Hz.

  1. What RPM would the engine need to spin for the fundamental firing frequency (1 × f_fire) itself to reach 1,050 Hz? Does the Cayman ever reach that RPM?

  2. Yet in reality, that 1,050 Hz formant lights up dramatically during a pull to 7,400 RPM! Calculate the exact three RPMs between 3,000 RPM and 7,400 RPM where the 6th, 4th, and 3rd harmonics of f_fire (6 f_fire, 4 f_fire, 3 f_fire) sweep straight across the 1,050 Hz peak!

Check Your Thinking

  1. Fundamental: (RPM / 20) = 1,050 ⇒ 21,000 RPM (late-2000s Formula 1 V8 territory!). A road-going 987.2 S redlines at 7,400 RPM (f_fire = 370 Hz), so the fundamental never gets anywhere near 1,050 Hz.

  2. Harmonic crossings: Because your source waveform contains integer harmonics of f_fire, each overtone marches through 1,050 Hz at a distinct RPM:

    • 6th harmonic (6 f_fire = 1,050 ⇒ f_fire = 175 Hz): Hits at 3,500 RPM.
    • 5th harmonic (5 f_fire = 1,050 ⇒ f_fire = 210 Hz): Hits at 4,200 RPM (right around VarioCam / intake flap transition!).
    • 4th harmonic (4 f_fire = 1,050 ⇒ f_fire = 262.5 Hz): Hits at 5,250 RPM.
    • 3rd harmonic (3 f_fire = 1,050 ⇒ f_fire = 350 Hz): Screams straight through the peak at 7,000 RPM right before redline!

This is why stationary formants sound magical: a single fixed filter creates a multi-stage evolving vocal "singing" across the rev range as successive harmonics climb the ladder through it!

1.5 Fixing the Physics-to-Audio Bridge: Drivetrain & Load at Parking Speeds

Even the best DSP sound engine will feel completely disconnected in index.html if you don't fix how GameEngine.js talks to AudioController.js.

Let's do quick math on your simulator's physical scale (from tests/unit/performance.test.js line 35: kmh = velocity / 20 * 3.6, meaning 20 pixels = 1 meter):

  • When you maneuver into a tight parallel or perpendicular spot, you drive at 2 to 8 km/h (11 to 44 px/s).
  • Compared to maxSpeed = 340 px/s (61.2 km/h, roughly top of 1st gear), your speedRatio during parking is 0.03 to 0.13.

If RPM is just idleRPM + speedRatio * (redline - idleRPM), three things feel wrong during parking:

Bug 1: Separating Braking from Reverse Engine Load

In GameEngine.js, pressing ArrowDown sets input.throttle = -1:

  • If the car is rolling forward (velocity > 2 px/s) and input.throttle < 0, the driver is pressing the footbrake, not the gas pedal! Engine load should be zero (closed throttle).
  • Once the car stops or rolls backward (velocity <= 2 px/s) and input.throttle < 0, the car is in Reverse gear and the driver is pressing the accelerator! Engine load should be Math.abs(input.throttle) (whereas your old Math.max(0, throttle) clamped reverse load to 0, making backing into a bay dead silent!).

Bug 2: Clutch Slip / Torque-Converter Flare & Flywheel Inertia

In a real car at 0 km/h, if the clutch were rigidly locked to the wheels, 0 km/h would mean 0 RPM (stalled!). Below ~10 km/h (55 px/s), a driver (or automatic torque converter / PDK clutch) slips the clutch:

  1. When you tap the gas from a standstill, the light flywheel spins up much faster than the 1,500 kg car—RPM flares immediately to 1,500–2,200 RPM ("blip/bite") while the car barely creeps at 3 km/h.
  2. As wheel speed catches up above ~10–12 km/h, wheelDrivenRPM takes over smoothly up to redline in 1st gear (or a shorter Reverse gear ratio, typically ~15% shorter than 1st!).
  3. You can model this cleanly with two coupled RPM targets and asymmetric flywheel inertia (dt smoothing):
    • freeRevTarget: idleRPM + engineThrottle * flareRange (e.g., flareRange ≈ 1400–1800 RPM when slipping from a stop).
    • coupledWheelRPM: (Math.abs(velocity) / maxGearSpeed) * redline.
    • targetRPM: Math.min(redline, Math.max(idleRPM, coupledWheelRPM, freeRevTarget)) (or a smooth blend as the clutch locks up!).
    • Flywheel smoothing: Engine RPM chases targetRPM faster when revving up under throttle (τ_up ≈ 0.08 s) than when decaying against internal friction (τ_down ≈ 0.22 s):
      rpm += (targetRPM - rpm) · (1 - e^-dt / τ)

Bug 3: Engine Load vs. Overrun (Engine Braking)

Pitch is controlled by RPM, but Timbre & Volume are controlled by Manifold Load:

  • High Load (engineThrottle > 0, climbing RPM): Throttle butterfly is wide open → loud intake roar, bright exhaust lowpass cutoff, high waveshaper drive, deep sub-bass.
  • Overrun / Deceleration (engineThrottle === 0 while rpm > idleRPM + 250): Throttle butterfly snaps shut! Intake roar drops to zero immediately, and exhaust lowpass closes down to a dark, hollow vacuum rumble (200–300 Hz).

Micro-Imperfection (Shared Crankshaft Hunting, Never Per-Order Detuning!)

At steady idle, a frozen 900.000 RPM sounds like an electrical utility transformer. Add ± 10–18 RPM of slow organic idle hunting (e.g., summing two slow incommensurate sine waves like 1.3 Hz and 3.1 Hz, tapered down as RPM rises) directly to the single shared crankshaft variable currentRPM so every engine order (1.0x, 1.5x, 3.0x, 6.0x) tracks together in rigid 0 cents phase-lock! Never apply static frequency offsets (such as 1.003x / 5 cents detune) between individual harmonic oscillators—doing so causes unphysical chorus warble at high RPM, whereas pre-waveshaper brown/pink gas turbulence already provides true cycle-to-cycle combustion texture.

1.6 Web Audio Smoothing & Gain Staging Tips

Two quick engineering guardrails before you jump into code:

  1. Always smooth 60 Hz parameter updates with setTargetAtTime: Your game loop updates audio 60 times/sec (every 16.7 ms), while audio plays at 48,000 samples/sec. Jumping filter.frequency.value = newFreq instantaneously creates audible clicks ("zipper noise"). You're already using param.setTargetAtTime(target, ctx.currentTime, timeConstant) in AudioController.js—keep using it! A timeConstant of 0.02 to 0.04 seconds reaches 63% of the target in 20–40 ms, eliminating clicks while feeling instantaneous to the driver's foot.

  2. Prevent Resonant Clipping: When a harmonic sits dead-on a +10 dB (3.16x amplitude!) formant peak, your signal level spikes—just as a real exhaust drones loudly at a specific RPM! Keep masterGain sensible (~0.35–0.5), or place a DynamicsCompressorNode (acting as a transparent brickwall safety limiter: threshold: -3, knee: 6, ratio: 12, attack: 0.003, release: 0.1) right before this.analyser so speaker outputs never hard-clip above 1.0.
