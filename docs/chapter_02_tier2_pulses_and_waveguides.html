2.1 Why Leave Oscillators Behind? The Duty-Cycle Problem

After completing Tier 1, your engines will already sound dramatically warmer and react properly to parking maneuvers. Yet if you listen closely at idle (850–950 RPM) or slow parking creep (1,200 RPM), you will still notice a subtle "synthesizer" smoothness. Why?

Because OscillatorNode generates a continuous, 100%-duty-cycle periodic wave:

Sawtooth Oscillator at 30 Hz (33 ms period):
  /|  /|  /|  /|     <-- Continuous ramp filling all 33 ms of every cycle
 / | / | / | / |

Real Cylinder Exhaust Port at 900 RPM (33 ms between firings):
 /\          /\      <-- Sharp 3 ms pressure explosion, followed by
   \________/  \___      ~30 ms of resonant pipe ringdown & near-silence!

At 850 RPM, your ear does not hear a continuous 28 Hz bass guitar note—human hearing resolves events separated by > 25 ms as discrete percussive chugs ("chug-chug-chug"). As RPM climbs past ~1,800 RPM (< 15 ms between pulses), those discrete explosions overlap in the exhaust pipe and naturally fuse into a continuous pitched roar!

Continuous Web Audio oscillators cannot:

  1. Change the duty cycle (ratio of pulse width to cycle period) as RPM rises.
  2. Fire unevenly spaced cylinder intervals (like one bank of a Cross-Plane V8).
  3. Vary individual explosions randomly from cycle to cycle (combustion variance and overrun pops).

To unlock all three at once, we step down from the 60 Hz Node Graph into the 48,000 Hz Sample Loop via AudioWorklet.

2.3 Crankshaft Phase Accumulator & 4-Stroke Geometry

Classic Beginner Bug: Never Multiply Frequency by Time!

When programmers first write a variable-pitch wave in a loop, they are tempted to write Math.sin(2 * Math.PI * freq * currentTime). Never do this when freq changes over time! By the product rule of calculus, the instantaneous frequency (derivative of phase 2π f(t) · t) becomes f(t) + t · (df / dt)—meaning after 60 seconds of play, tapping the gas multiplies pitch wildly by 60×!

Instead, just like integrating pos += velocity * dt in GameEngine.js, you integrate crankshaft angle θ incrementally by a tiny step Δθ each sample:

Δθ = 2π · (RPM / 60 · f_s) (radians per sample)

Because a 4-stroke Otto cycle (Intake → Compression → Power → Exhaust) takes two full crankshaft revolutions, our repeating cycle wraps at 4π radians (720°), not 2π:

θ[n] = (θ[n-1] + Δθ) mod 4π

Each cylinder k ∈ 0 … N_cyl-1 has a fixed Exhaust Valve Opening (EVO) trigger angle θ_k ∈ [0, 4π).

Wrap-Safe Trigger Detection & Free Sub-Sample Accuracy

How do you know if cylinder k just opened its exhaust valve on the current sample? Compute the wrapped angular distance ϕ_k ∈ [0, 4π) ahead of cylinder k's trigger angle:

ϕ_k = (θ - θ_k + 4π) mod 4π
  • If ϕ_k < Δθ, the crankshaft just crossed θ_k during this exact sample!
  • Even better: Δ t_sub = (ϕ_k / Δθ · f_s) tells you the exact sub-sample fraction of a sample ago that the valve cracked open! Starting your pulse envelope at t = Δ t_sub instead of t = 0 eliminates high-RPM sample-grid quantization buzz (aliasing) in two lines of arithmetic!

  2. At 7,200 RPM, by how many degrees does the crankshaft rotate in a single audio sample? Why would checking if (Math.abs(theta - theta_k) < 0.0001) skip almost every cylinder firing?

Check Your Thinking

  1. Samples per 720° cycle:

    • At 900 RPM: f_rot = 15 rev/s ⇒ 7.5 full 720° cycles/sec. (48,000 / 7.5) = 6,400 samples per 4-stroke cycle.
    • At 7,200 RPM: f_rot = 120 rev/s ⇒ 60 full 720° cycles/sec. (48,000 / 60) = 800 samples per 4-stroke cycle.

  2. Degrees per sample at 7,200 RPM:

    • (720° / 800 samples) = 0.9° per sample (Δθ ≈ 0.0157 rad).
    • Because θ leaps by 0.0157 rad each sample, it will almost never land within 0.0001 of theta_k—your engine would misfire 99% of the time! Checking phi_k < dTheta catches 100% of crossings across every RPM, including right across the 4π → 0 boundary.

2.4 Does Each Pulse Have Sound? Synthesizing a Single Exhaust Blowdown

Now for your central Tier 2 question: When a single cylinder fires, what sound does one isolated pulse make, and how do we generate it mathematically?

Yes—each cylinder event is literally a miniature uncorked champagne pop inside the exhaust port! Let's build up the exact waveform of a single pulse in three steps so you see why naive pulses fail and why a physical blowdown wavelet works wonders.

Attempt 1 (Naive): The 1-Sample Digital Spike ([1, 0, 0, 0...])

What if you output 1.0 on the trigger sample and 0.0 everywhere else? In DSP, a 1-sample spike (0.02 ms wide) is a Dirac Delta Impulse. Its Fourier transform is a perfectly flat horizontal line: it contains equal energy at 50 Hz, 1,000 Hz, 10,000 Hz, and 24,000 Hz! A train of 1-sample clicks sounds like an electric taser or Geiger counter, not a combustion engine.

Attempt 2 (Flawed): A Purely Positive Pressure Hump

What if each valve opening outputs a smooth 2 ms positive bump p(t) ≥ 0? Because every sample is positive, the average value (DC offset) is strictly greater than zero—and as RPM climbs from 900 to 7,000 RPM, 8× as many positive bumps pack into each second! That rising DC offset shoves your speaker cone steadily outward toward 1.0, eating up all your headroom and distorting asymmetrically.

Attempt 3 (Physical Reality): The Zero-DC Biphasic Blowdown Wave + Turbulent Chuff

Look at the actual gas dynamics when an exhaust valve cracks open (~45° before Bottom Dead Center):

  1. The Compression Front (0 → 0.5 ms): Residual cylinder pressure (5–8 bar at full throttle; ~ 1.3 bar at idle) blasts past the valve seat, launching a steep positive pressure crest down the header primary.
  2. Scavenging Rarefaction (0.5 ms → 4 ms): Momentum of the fast-moving gas column over-expands the port (exhaust scavenging!), creating a broader, shallower negative pressure trough behind the crest. Furthermore, when that pulse exits the open tailpipe into outside air, acoustic radiation pressure is proportional to the time derivative of volume velocity (p_rad ∝ (dU / dt))—meaning a radiated acoustic pulse always has equal positive and negative area (zero DC offset)!
  3. Turbulent Shear "Chuff": Supersonic gas shearing through the narrow valve gap injects a brief puff of band-limited turbulent flow noise riding on the crest of the pulse.
Pressure p(t) of ONE Cylinder Exhaust Blowdown Pulse:

  +1.0 |    /\      <-- Steep positive blowdown shock (+ turbulent gas noise)
       |   /  \
   0.0 +--+----\----------------------> time t (ms)
       |        \      ________
 -0.34 |         \____/        ^-- Broader scavenging rarefaction trough
                                   (Positive area == Negative area -> Zero DC!)

A Clean, Exact Equation You Can Code in 4 Lines

Consider the normalized dimensionless wavelet w(u) where u = (t / τ_0) ≥ 0, controlled by a single characteristic blowdown time constant τ_0 (in seconds):

w(u) = 2.17 · u(2 - u)e^-u for 0 ≤ u ≤ 10 (and 0 for u > 10)

Figure 2.1: Single-Cylinder Exhaust Blowdown Pressure Wavelet w(t) (Zero Net DC Offset)

Look at the properties this tiny formula gives you for free:

  • Zero click at t = 0: w(0) = 0 (ramps up smoothly as the valve cracks open).
  • Asymmetric blowdown punch: Peaks sharply at u ≈ 0.59 (w ≈ +1.0), crosses zero at u = 2.0, dips into a wide scavenging trough of -0.34 at u ≈ 3.41, and decays to < 0.008 by u = 10.

  • Mathematically guaranteed Zero DC Offset at every RPM:

    ∫_0^∞ u(2 - u)e^-u du = 2∫_0^∞ u e^-u du - ∫_0^∞ u^2 e^-u du = 2(1!) - 2! = 2 - 2 = 0

  • Adding Turbulent Gas Chuff: Mix in a touch of filtered white noise gated by the positive blowdown crest (u < 2) and scaled by load:

    p_total(u) = A_load · [ w(u) + k_turb · load · max(0, w(u)) · noise[n] ]

How Load & RPM Shape τ_0 Naturally

Why does an engine bark sharply under full throttle, purr softly at idle, and sound tight at high RPM? Because τ_0 changes physically!

  • At Idle / Low Load (load ≈ 0.05): Low cylinder blowdown pressure ⇒ lazy, wide pulse (τ_0 ≈ 0.9–1.3 ms, total tail ~ 10 ms). Smooth, wide pulses naturally lack harsh high frequencies!
  • Under Full Throttle (load = 1.0) & High RPM: High cylinder pressure + fast valve opening ⇒ crisp, narrow shock (τ_0 ≈ 0.25–0.45 ms, total tail ~ 3 ms) with 4–6× larger amplitude A_load. Narrower pulses automatically pack punchy mid/high harmonics into the headers!

Superposition (When Pulses Overlap at High RPM)

Each cylinder k tracks the elapsed time t_k since its most recent EVO trigger (if active, i.e., t_k < 10τ_0). At each sample, you simply advance t_k += 1 / sampleRate for each cylinder and sum their active pressures into the manifold! Zero dynamic arrays needed—just a fixed loop of length N_cyl ≤ 8.

Pause & Think 2.2: Pulse Width vs. Brightness

Without writing any filter at all, imagine listening to two isolated pulses: - Pulse A has τ_0 = 1.2 ms (positive crest lasts 2τ_0 = 2.4 ms). - Pulse B has τ_0 = 0.3 ms (positive crest lasts 2τ_0 = 0.6 ms). Roughly what half-wave sine frequency corresponds to a 2.4 ms crest vs. a 0.6 ms crest? Why does shrinking τ_0 when you floor the accelerator pedal automatically brighten the engine's timbre before any EQ filter?

Check Your Thinking
  • A half-cycle lasting 2.4 ms corresponds to a full period of 4.8 ms ⇒ f ≈ (1 / 0.0048) ≈ 208 Hz! Energy above a few hundred Hz rolls off naturally because a wide, gentle wave cannot wiggle fast enough to excite 2 kHz air vibrations.
  • Shrinking the crest 4× to 0.6 ms corresponds to a full period of 1.2 ms ⇒ f ≈ 833 Hz, extending strong harmonic energy up past 2,000 Hz!
  • In physics and Fourier analysis, this is the Time–Frequency Scaling Property: compressing a pulse in time by 4× stretches its frequency spectrum upward by 4×! That means load-dependent τ_0 gives you organic harmonic brightening straight from thermodynamics.

2.5 Firing Orders, Bank Geometry & Why V8s Burble While Flat-6s Howl

Figure 2.2: Cross-Plane V8 Uneven Bank Spacing vs. Flat-6 Even Spacing Across 720° Cycle

Here is one of the coolest payoffs in procedural audio: once you model per-cylinder pulses and dual-bank manifolds, engine character emerges directly from real mechanical blueprints!

Let's compare the 720° (4π) crank trigger schedules across your presets:

1. Inline-4 (i4, civic_vtec) — Firing Order 1-3-4-2

  • EVO Angles: [0°, 180°, 360°, 540°] (even π rad spacing into a single 4-into-1 collector).
  • Acoustic signature: Strong 2E pulse train. (Fun experiment: if you add 35 cm of extra runner pipe delay to two cylinders on one side, your I4 instantly transforms into a Subaru WRX unequal-length Boxer-4 off-beat rumble!)

2. BMW Straight-6 (i6) — Firing Order 1-5-3-6-2-4

  • Bank / Manifold split:
    • Front Manifold (Cyl 1, 3, 2): Fires at 0°, 240°, 480° (240° spacing).
    • Rear Manifold (Cyl 5, 6, 4): Fires at 120°, 360°, 600° (240° spacing, alternating Front-Rear-Front-Rear every 120°!).
  • Why BMW splits 1-2-3 and 4-5-6: Grouping Front 3 and Rear 3 into separate downpipes prevents adjacent blowdown pulses from spoiling cylinder scavenging before they merge downstream at the mid-pipe resonator with a slight 15–30 cm path difference.

3. Porsche 987.2 S Flat-6 (flat6) — Firing Order 1-6-2-4-3-5

  • Bank split:
    • Left Bank (Cyl 1, 2, 3): Fires at 0° (Cyl 1), 240° (Cyl 2), 480° (Cyl 3).
    • Right Bank (Cyl 6, 4, 5): Fires at 120° (Cyl 6), 360° (Cyl 4), 600° (Cyl 5).
  • Why a Cayman sounds completely different from a single-pipe 6-cylinder: Left and Right banks travel through two independent headers, catalytic converters, and muffler chambers, meeting only at an acoustic balance tube / dual center tailpipe! If Left and Right pipes reach the listener's ears with even a tiny acoustic path asymmetry (0.3–0.8 ms, or stereo panning!), the 1.5E bank pulses and 3E combined pulses create that unmistakable hollow boxer thrum at 2,000 RPM that locks into a spine-tingling 6-cylinder wail above 4,500 RPM!

4. Cross-Plane V8 (v8) — Classic Firing Order 1-8-4-3-6-5-7-2

Look closely at which bank (L = Left 1,3,5,7; R = Right 2,4,6,8) fires every 90°:

Crank Angle 0° 90° 180° 270° 360° 450° 540° 630°
Cylinder 1 8 4 3 6 5 7 2
Bank L R R L R L L R

Look at Left Bank alone: it fires at 0° → 270° → 450° → 540° → (720°). The angular gaps inside the Left exhaust pipe are:

270° - 180° - 90° - 180°

Two cylinders (5 then 7 on Left; 8 then 4 on Right) fire back-to-back 90° apart ("double-hit"), followed by a huge 270° pause in that pipe! That L - R - R - L - R - L - L - R bank bunching is 100% of why an American / Land Cruiser Cross-Plane V8 burbles like a motorboat, whereas a Ferrari Flat-Plane V8 (L - R - L - R - L - R - L - R) screams smoothly like two synced Inline-4s!

  1. Instantaneous identical sum collapses the burble! Because {0°, 270°, 450°, 540°} ∪ {90°, 180°, 360°, 630°} equals {0°, 90°, 180°, 270°, 360°, 450°, 540°, 630°}, summing two identical linear banks with zero delay/nonlinearity erases the bank distinction and leaves a completely uniform 90° (4E) hum!

Two pulses bunched 90° apart inside the Left header overlap and saturate differently than two pulses split across Left and Right pipes.
    • Acoustic Path-Length Asymmetry (and/or Stereo Panning): On virtually every V8 (especially transverse routing around a transmission/transfer case like a Land Cruiser, or standing slightly off-axis from dual tailpipes), one bank's exhaust path to the listener is 25–70 cm (0.7–2.0 ms, or 35–95 samples) longer than the other, and in headphones you can pan Left Bank slightly left (-0.25) and Right Bank slightly right (+0.25)!

2.6 1D Acoustic Waveguides: Putting a Metal Pipe Around Your Pulses

Figure 2.3: 1D Acoustic Digital Waveguide with Inverting Open Tailpipe Reflection

If you listen to your raw zero-DC blowdown pulses directly, they sound like an un-piped lawnmower cylinder firing into open air—percussive, crisp, and engine-like, but missing the hollow, metallic "tubiness" of headers and tailpipes.

How do we simulate a 2 m steel exhaust pipe at 48,000 Hz without solving partial differential equations? With a 1D Digital Waveguide—which is just a circular delay buffer (Float32Array) + a 1-line reflection filter!

Step 1: Pipe Length = Sample Delay (N)

Sound in hot exhaust gas (~ 350–500°C) travels much faster than in room-temperature air (343 m/s) because speed of sound scales with √T_Kelvin:

c_exhaust ≈ 480 m/s

For a pulse to travel down a pipe of length L meters and reflect back to the collector (a round trip of 2L meters), the delay in audio samples is:

N_RT = round\!((2L / c_exhaust) · f_s)

For example, a 1.0 m pipe at c = 480 m/s and f_s = 48,000 Hz is a round-trip delay of just N_RT = 200 floats in a circular Float32Array! (In code, you don't shift array elements every sample: you keep one integer cursor idx, read the oldest sample const delayed = buf[idx], overwrite buf[idx] = newSample in the exact same slot, and advance idx = (idx + 1) % N_RT—an O(1) delay line!)

Step 2: Why an Open Tailpipe Reflects Waves Upside Down (R < 0)

Here is a classic acoustics surprise: how can sound reflect off the open end of an empty tailpipe when there is no wall there?

Inside the narrow steel pipe, the gas pulse is confined by rigid walls (high acoustic impedance). The instant it hits the open tailpipe exit, it suddenly expands into the vast 3D atmosphere (low acoustic impedance), clamping gauge pressure toward 0. By momentum conservation, that sudden pressure drop sucks a negative (sign-inverted) rarefaction wave (R ≈ -0.55 to -0.72) backward up the tailpipe toward the engine!

Step 3: High-Frequency Wall Losses (1-Pole Low-Pass in the Loop)

Higher frequencies dissipate faster against muffler perforations and pipe walls than low bass. Inside an AudioWorklet, you don't have BiquadFilterNode, so you write the simplest filter in all of DSP—a 1-pole Exponential Moving Average (EMA):

lpState = (1 - d) * delayed + d * lpState;
  • Why is a weighted average a low-pass filter? For a slow 80 Hz bass wave, adjacent samples at 48 kHz are nearly identical, so lpState tracks the wave 1:1. For a rapid 12 kHz hiss (+1, -1, +1, -1), blending the new sample with previous opposite-sign samples averages it out toward 0!
  • How to pick d without guessing: Given a desired wall-loss cutoff frequency f_wall in Hz (e.g., 1,800–3,500 Hz):
    d = e^-2π · f_wall / f_s

Wrapping the pipe sum in Math.tanh(x) adds realistic high-pressure gas compression and guarantees the waveguide can never blow up.

(Bonus Header Detail: You can also give each individual cylinder primary runner a tiny non-feedback delay line of 35–75 cm before summing into the Bank Waveguide! Varying those 4 or 6 runner lengths by even 3–8 cm gives realistic collector phase texture.)

2.7 Breathing Life Into the Machine: Jitter, Intake Gulp & Overrun Burbles

With crank pulses, dual-bank geometry, and 1D pipe waveguides working, three final micro-details elevate Tier 2 from "impressive simulation" to visceral:

1. Cycle-to-Cycle Combustion Stochasticity

In a real cylinder, air-fuel mixing and spark kernel growth vary slightly on every single explosion. Whenever cylinder k crosses its EVO trigger (phi_k < dTheta), randomize that individual pulse's amplitude and width slightly:

  • Pulse Amplitude Jitter: A_k = A_base · (1 + rand(-0.07, +0.07)) (wider variance ± 12% at idle when combustion is cooler/lazier; tighter ± 4% under full load).
  • Pulse Width Jitter: τ_k = τ_0 · (1 + rand(-0.05, +0.05)). Because every cylinder stroke excites the waveguide slightly differently, the engine never sounds "looped" even if you let it idle for 5 minutes!

2. Intake Induction Pulses (Why Wide-Open Throttle Growls From the Front)

Intake sound isn't just continuous white-noise hiss—every time an Intake Valve Opens (IVO) (360° = 2π rad offset from EVO) and the piston plunges down, it sucks a sharp negative pressure gulp out of the intake plenum!

  • Crucial physical gate: Between the intake plenum and outside air sits the throttle butterfly valve.
    • When load === 0 (closed throttle), the metal throttle plate blocks the sound from escaping out the snorkel ⇒ intake pulse gain ≈ 0.
    • As load -> 1.0 (wide-open throttle), you get an unobstructed acoustic shot straight down the intake runners to the intake valves! Feeding those IVO pulses into your Tier 1 parallel Intake Formants (F_airbox + F_runner) produces true induction bark (especially on the 987.2 S Cayman and B16 Civic VTEC!).

3. Lift-Off Overrun Burbles

When you rev past 2,800 RPM and suddenly snap the throttle shut (load === 0, isOverrun === true), manifold vacuum spikes and tiny pockets of unburnt fuel ignite late in the hot exhaust manifold:

  • During overrun, normal cylinder pulses become very soft and wide (low pressure, large τ_0 ≈ 1.3 ms).
  • With a small per-firing probability (say p = 0.04 to 0.12 while rpm > 2500 on sporty presets like m3_g80 or cayman_987, tapering to 0 near idle), trigger a single sharper, medium-amplitude delayed pop inside one bank's waveguide! Because it passes through the exact same exhaust pipe waveguide and formant filters, it sounds like an organic exhaust burble rather than a canned sound effect.

Check Your Thinking

  1. Intake channel: Drops immediately to near-silence within 20–30 ms even though the engine is still spinning at 5,400 RPM, because the throttle butterfly plate physically slammed shut!

  2. Exhaust channel:

    • Pulse rate: Still firing at the 5,400 RPM rate (270 pulses/sec) as the car's momentum/flywheel spins the crank!
    • Pulse amplitude A & width τ_0: Because fuel is cut (or minimal) and manifold pressure is deep vacuum, cylinder blowdown pressure plummets—so A drops to ~15–20% of full-throttle level and τ_0 widens from ~0.3 ms to ~1.1 ms (plus occasional random overrun burble pops), producing that smooth, hollow, decelerating engine-braking purr down the rev range!
